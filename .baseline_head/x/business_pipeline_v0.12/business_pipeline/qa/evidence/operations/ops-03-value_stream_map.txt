A value stream map follows one flow from request to delivery: the steps in order, the time each takes, what waits between them, which steps the customer would pay for, and the improvements proposed against a measure. Each element is treated in turn, first through the evidence accepted for the case and then through what that evidence implies for the decision.
Process Steps
The lunch service at the meal counter takes an order, assembles and packs the meal, then the student collects and pays.
The steps are what happens to the offer between request and delivery. Where they are stated in order, the flow becomes testable: a step outside the map is outside the scope of the improvement. The relevant question is whether the stated order is the one the offer actually follows.
Cycle and Lead Times
No evidence was found for this part, and the instruction does not describe it. What to state: state the times you observed or were given, naming the step each one belongs to.
Times turn a sequence into a flow. A cycle time is what one step takes for one unit; a lead time is what the whole flow takes for one order. Where both are declared, the part of the lead time spent waiting can be located. The relevant question is whether the declared times describe a normal period rather than a best case.
Inventory and Work in Progress
No evidence was found for this part, and the instruction does not describe it. What to state: state what waits between the steps, and how much of it.
Inventory between steps is work that has been received or started and not yet passed on. It protects the next step from variability and ties up cash at the same time. The relevant question is whether the declared stock is what the plan can afford to hold.
Waits and Delays
At noon the queue reaches the gate, one oven bakes one tray at a time, and the supervisor re-times the wait from collection.
A wait is where the flow stops: a queue in front of a step, a batch that must be completed before the next step starts, a hand-off or an approval. Where a wait is declared, the improvement has somewhere to act. The relevant question is which declared wait accounts for most of the lead time.
Value-Added Assessment
This part is a judgement or a proposal only you can state, so it is left for you and never filled by the pipeline. What to state: judge each step yourself: the customer pays for the steps that change the offer, and this judgement is yours to state and to defend.
The value-added judgement separates the steps the customer pays for from the steps the process needs. It is a judgement about the offer, not a measurement, and two analysts can defend different answers on the same flow.
Improvement Opportunities
This part is a judgement or a proposal only you can state, so it is left for you and never filled by the pipeline. What to state: propose the changes yourself, each with the measure that would show whether it worked.
An improvement is a declared change with a declared measure. Without the measure the change cannot be shown to have worked; without the declaration the change cannot be reviewed. The relevant question is whether the measure already exists in the flow.
Discussion
What the Evidence Shows About the Flow
The case supplies 2 accepted items across the elements: 0 sourced items, 2 items of the student's own evidence and 0 case assumptions. The elements carrying evidence are Process steps (1), Waits and delays (1), while 4 element(s) are recorded as gaps.
Read together, the elements describe a flow in which some conditions are evidenced and others are not, and an element without accepted evidence cannot be treated as favourable. An element without evidence carries no weight in the assessment, because a gap is not a finding and cannot be read as either a strength or a weakness.
Implications for the Decision the Improvement Must Serve
The combined reading bears on the decision: the documented conditions must be answered by the proposed position, and the plan must show how each would be met. An element that appears weak in the evidence is not thereby one that can be disregarded; it is an element the case has not documented.
An improvement decision has to answer the waits the evidence shows: the time a step takes must be the time the plan assumes, the inventory between steps must be affordable, and any change must be judged against a measure the flow already has. The decision itself remains the student's.
Limitations
The analysis does not rate the elements, compute an aggregate score or recommend a decision, because the accepted items do not support a quantitative comparison across them. Several elements may rest on a single item, which limits the weight they can bear; an assumption is not a source and a gap is not a finding. The student must verify every item before submission.
Conclusion
The analysis set out to establish, element by element, what the accepted evidence implies for the decision. The elements with evidence are Process steps (1), Waits and delays (1). Each of them carries the same number of accepted items, so on this evidence none is better supported than another.
The proposition advanced in the introduction is supported only to the extent that the items reach. The paper does not make the decision; it identifies the conditions that any justification must address.
