Introduction
The decision under examination is whether to proceed with the course of action set out in the brief. The unit of analysis is one plan, because S&amp;OP reconciles the functions of one product or line rather than the firm as a whole. The evidence base is confined to the items accepted for the case.
The analysis treats each of the 6 plans in turn: demand plan, supply plan, inventory plan, capacity plan, financial plan, gaps and decisions. Each is established from the accepted evidence and then interpreted, with attention to its effect on the decision.
The argument advanced is that the plans supported by evidence constrain the decision, and that a plan for which no evidence is recorded cannot be treated as favourable. The conclusion returns to this proposition.
The Plan and Its Scope
The case concerns the following decision: The lunch service at the meal counter takes an order, assembles and packs the meal, then the student collects and pays. At noon the queue reaches the gate, one oven bakes one tray at a time, and the supervisor re-times the wait from collection. The counter hands on a packed meal and a payment receipt. Bread arrives from one regional bakery every morning before opening. The relevant plan is the one named in the brief; the plans are properties of that product or line rather than of the organisation.
Evidence is limited to the accepted items. A plan without an accepted item is recorded as a gap, and an assumption is identified as such and is not treated as evidence. No aggregate score or recommendation is produced.

Discussion
What the Evidence Shows About the Plan
The case supplies 6 accepted items across the plans: 6 sourced items, 0 items of the student's own evidence and 0 case assumptions. The plans carrying evidence are Demand plan (1), Supply plan (1), Inventory plan (1), Capacity plan (1), Financial plan (1), Gaps and decisions (1), while 0 plan(s) are recorded as gaps.
Read together, the plans describe a decision in which some figures are evidenced and others are not, and a plan without evidence cannot be treated as agreed. A plan without evidence carries no weight in the assessment, because a gap is not a finding and cannot be read as either a strength or a weakness.
Implications for the Decision the Plan Requires
The combined reading bears on the decision: the documented conditions must be answered by the proposed position, and the plan must show how each would be met. A plan that appears weak in the evidence is not thereby one that can be disregarded; it is a plan the case has not documented.
A decision has to reconcile the plans the evidence shows: demand must be met by supply, capacity must exist where it binds, and the financial result must survive. The decision itself remains the student's.
Limitations
The analysis does not rate the plans, compute an aggregate score or recommend a decision, because the accepted items do not support a quantitative comparison across them. Several plans may rest on a single item, which limits the weight they can bear; an assumption is not a source and a gap is not a finding. The student must verify every item before submission.
Conclusion
The analysis set out to establish, plan by plan, what the accepted evidence implies for the decision. The plans with evidence are Demand plan (1), Supply plan (1), Inventory plan (1), Capacity plan (1), Financial plan (1), Gaps and decisions (1). Each of them carries the same number of accepted items, so on this evidence none is better supported than another.
The proposition advanced in the introduction is supported only to the extent that the items reach. The paper does not make the decision; it identifies the conditions that any justification must address.
