Introduction
The decision under examination is whether to proceed with the course of action set out in the brief. The unit of analysis is one change, because PDCA tests a specific intervention in a specific flow rather than a programme. The evidence base is confined to the items accepted for the case.
The analysis treats each of the 4 stages in turn: plan, do, check, act. Each is established from the accepted evidence and then interpreted, with attention to its effect on the decision.
The argument advanced is that the stages supported by evidence constrain the decision, and that a stage for which no evidence is recorded cannot be treated as favourable. The conclusion returns to this proposition.
The Change and Where It Is Tried
The case concerns the following decision: The lunch service at the meal counter takes an order, assembles and packs the meal, then the student collects and pays. At noon the queue reaches the gate, one oven bakes one tray at a time, and the supervisor re-times the wait from collection. The counter hands on a packed meal and a payment receipt. Bread arrives from one regional bakery every morning before opening. The relevant change is the one named in the brief; the stages are properties of that trial rather than of the organisation.
Evidence is limited to the accepted items. A stage without an accepted item is recorded as a gap, and an assumption is identified as such and is not treated as evidence. No aggregate score or recommendation is produced.

Discussion
What the Evidence Shows About the Change
The case supplies 4 accepted items across the stages: 4 sourced items, 0 items of the student's own evidence and 0 case assumptions. The stages carrying evidence are Plan (1), Do (1), Check (1), Act (1), while 0 stage(s) are recorded as gaps.
Read together, the stages describe a trial in which some conditions are evidenced and others are not, and a stage without evidence cannot be treated as complete. A stage without evidence carries no weight in the assessment, because a gap is not a finding and cannot be read as either a strength or a weakness.
Implications for the Decision the Result Supports
The combined reading bears on the decision: the documented conditions must be answered by the proposed position, and the plan must show how each would be met. A stage that appears weak in the evidence is not thereby one that can be disregarded; it is a stage the case has not documented.
A decision to standardise has to answer the stages the evidence shows: the plan must state a target, the check must measure it, and the act must follow the result. The decision itself remains the student's.
Limitations
The analysis does not rate the stages, compute an aggregate score or recommend a decision, because the accepted items do not support a quantitative comparison across them. Several stages may rest on a single item, which limits the weight they can bear; an assumption is not a source and a gap is not a finding. The student must verify every item before submission.
Conclusion
The analysis set out to establish, stage by stage, what the accepted evidence implies for the decision. The stages with evidence are Plan (1), Do (1), Check (1), Act (1). Each of them carries the same number of accepted items, so on this evidence none is better supported than another.
The proposition advanced in the introduction is supported only to the extent that the items reach. The paper does not make the decision; it identifies the conditions that any justification must address.
