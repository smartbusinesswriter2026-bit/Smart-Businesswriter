Introduction
The decision under examination is whether to proceed with the course of action set out in the brief. The unit of analysis is one problem in one process, because a cause is only a cause of a specific effect in a specific flow. The evidence base is confined to the items accepted for the case.
The analysis treats each of the 8 categories in turn: problem, people, process / method, equipment, materials, measurement, environment, root cause. Each is established from the accepted evidence and then interpreted, with attention to its effect on the decision.
The argument advanced is that the categories supported by evidence constrain the decision, and that a category for which no evidence is recorded cannot be treated as favourable. The conclusion returns to this proposition.
The Process and the Problem
The case concerns the following decision: The lunch service at the meal counter takes an order, assembles and packs the meal, then the student collects and pays. At noon the queue reaches the gate, one oven bakes one tray at a time, and the supervisor re-times the wait from collection. The counter hands on a packed meal and a payment receipt. Bread arrives from one regional bakery every morning before opening. The relevant process is the one named in the brief; the categories are properties of that problem rather than of the organisation.
Evidence is limited to the accepted items. A category without an accepted item is recorded as a gap, and an assumption is identified as such and is not treated as evidence. No aggregate score or recommendation is produced.
Root Cause Analysis

Discussion
What the Evidence Shows About the Causes
The case supplies 8 accepted items across the categories: 8 sourced items, 0 items of the student's own evidence and 0 case assumptions. The categories carrying evidence are Problem (1), People (1), Process / method (1), Equipment (1), Materials (1), Measurement (1), Environment (1), Root cause (1), while 0 category(s) are recorded as gaps.
Read together, the categories describe a problem in which some causes are evidenced and others are not, and a category without evidence cannot be treated as cleared. A category without evidence carries no weight in the assessment, because a gap is not a finding and cannot be read as either a strength or a weakness.
Implications for the Decision the Cause Supports
The combined reading bears on the decision: the documented conditions must be answered by the proposed position, and the plan must show how each would be met. A category that appears weak in the evidence is not thereby one that can be disregarded; it is a category the case has not documented.
A remedy has to answer the cause the evidence supports: a cause that is only a hypothesis may be worth testing, but it is not yet a reason to change the process. The decision itself remains the student's.
Limitations
The analysis does not rate the categories, compute an aggregate score or recommend a decision, because the accepted items do not support a quantitative comparison across them. Several categories may rest on a single item, which limits the weight they can bear; an assumption is not a source and a gap is not a finding. The student must verify every item before submission.
