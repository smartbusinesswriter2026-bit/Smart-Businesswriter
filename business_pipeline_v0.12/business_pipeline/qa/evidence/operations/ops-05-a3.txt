Introduction
The decision under examination is whether to proceed with the course of action set out in the brief. The unit of analysis is the process the problem sits in, because an A3 is a statement about one gap in one flow rather than about the organisation as a whole. The evidence base is confined to the items accepted for the case.
The analysis treats each of the 7 sections in turn: problem, current condition, target condition, root causes, countermeasures, plan and owners, follow-up. Each is established from the accepted evidence and then interpreted, with attention to its effect on the decision.
The argument advanced is that the sections supported by evidence constrain the decision, and that a section for which no evidence is recorded cannot be treated as favourable. The conclusion returns to this proposition.
The Process and the Problem
The case concerns the following decision: The lunch service at the meal counter takes an order, assembles and packs the meal, then the student collects and pays. At noon the queue reaches the gate, one oven bakes one tray at a time, and the supervisor re-times the wait from collection. The counter hands on a packed meal and a payment receipt. Bread arrives from one regional bakery every morning before opening. The relevant process is the one named in the brief; the sections are properties of that problem rather than of the firm.
Evidence is limited to the accepted items. A section without an accepted item is recorded as a gap, and an assumption is identified as such and is not treated as evidence. No aggregate score or recommendation is produced.

Discussion
What the Evidence Shows About the Problem
The case supplies 7 accepted items across the sections: 7 sourced items, 0 items of the student's own evidence and 0 case assumptions. The sections carrying evidence are Problem (1), Current condition (1), Target condition (1), Root causes (1), Countermeasures (1), Plan and owners (1), Follow-up (1), while 0 section(s) are recorded as gaps.
Read together, the sections describe a problem in which some conditions are evidenced and others are not, and a section without accepted evidence cannot be treated as settled. A section without evidence carries no weight in the assessment, because a gap is not a finding and cannot be read as either a strength or a weakness.
Implications for the Decision the Evidence Supports
The combined reading bears on the decision: the documented conditions must be answered by the proposed position, and the plan must show how each would be met. A section that appears weak in the evidence is not thereby one that can be disregarded; it is a section the case has not documented.
A countermeasure has to answer the cause the evidence shows: the current condition must be the one the gap describes, the cause must be supported rather than assumed, and the follow-up must measure the effect the target names. The decision itself remains the student's.
Limitations
The analysis does not rate the sections, compute an aggregate score or recommend a decision, because the accepted items do not support a quantitative comparison across them. Several sections may rest on a single item, which limits the weight they can bear; an assumption is not a source and a gap is not a finding. The student must verify every item before submission.
Conclusion
The analysis set out to establish, section by section, what the accepted evidence implies for the decision. The sections with evidence are Problem (1), Current condition (1), Target condition (1), Root causes (1), Countermeasures (1), Plan and owners (1), Follow-up (1). Each of them carries the same number of accepted items, so on this evidence none is better supported than another.
The proposition advanced in the introduction is supported only to the extent that the items reach. The paper does not make the decision; it identifies the conditions that any justification must address.
