Introduction
The decision under examination is whether to proceed with the course of action set out in the brief. The unit of analysis is the industry rather than the firm, because the value chain describes the activities any competitor must perform. The evidence base is confined to the items accepted for the case.
The analysis treats each of the 9 activities in turn: firm infrastructure, human resource management, technology development, procurement, inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing and sales, service. Each is established from the accepted evidence and then interpreted, with attention to its effect on the decision.
The argument advanced is that the activities supported by evidence constrain the decision, and that an activity for which no evidence is recorded cannot be treated as favourable. The conclusion returns to this proposition.
Case and Market Context
The case concerns the following decision: A grocery retailer is reviewing how its value chain creates value and where cost and differentiation come from, before deciding whether to open a new store format. The relevant industry is the one named in the brief; the activities are properties of that industry rather than of a single firm.
Evidence is limited to the accepted items. An activity without an accepted item is recorded as a gap, and an assumption is identified as such and is not treated as evidence. No aggregate score or recommendation is produced.

Discussion
What the Evidence Shows
The case supplies 9 accepted items across the activities: 0 sourced items, 0 items of the student's own evidence and 9 case assumptions. The activities carrying evidence are Firm infrastructure (1), Human resource management (1), Technology development (1), Procurement (1), Inbound logistics (1), Operations (1), Outbound logistics (1), Marketing and sales (1), Service (1), while 0 activity(s) are recorded as gaps.
Read together, the activities describe an industry in which some are evidenced and others are not, and an activity without evidence carries no weight. An activity without evidence carries no weight in the assessment, because a gap is not a finding and cannot be read as either a strength or a weakness.
Implications for the Decision
The combined reading bears on the decision: the documented conditions must be answered by the proposed position, and the plan must show how each would be met. An activity that appears weak in the evidence is not thereby one that can be disregarded; it is a activity the case has not documented.
A position must be built on the activities the evidence shows: the ones that create value and the ones that destroy it. The decision itself remains the student's.
Limitations
The analysis does not rate the activities, compute an aggregate score or recommend a decision, because the accepted items do not support a quantitative comparison across them. Several activities may rest on a single item, which limits the weight they can bear; an assumption is not a source and a gap is not a finding. The student must verify every item before submission.
Conclusion
The analysis set out to establish, activity by activity, what the accepted evidence implies for the decision. The activities with evidence are Firm infrastructure (1), Human resource management (1), Technology development (1), Procurement (1), Inbound logistics (1), Operations (1), Outbound logistics (1), Marketing and sales (1), Service (1), and Firm infrastructure carries the most weight in the reading offered here, while Firm infrastructure carries the least.
The proposition advanced in the introduction is supported only to the extent that the items reach. The paper does not make the decision; it identifies the conditions that any justification must address.
